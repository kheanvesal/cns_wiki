# Porte (2001) — typographical conditions and L2 error salience in proofreading

## Citation
Porte, G. (2001). Losing sight of errors: the effects of typographical conditions on error salience in L2 proof-reading. *System*, 29(2), 137–148. PII S0346-251X(00)00050-6.

## Reading task(s)
**proofreading** — error **recognition** only, not correction.

## Question / aim
Does artificially manipulating the typographical conditions in which text is proof-read promote better **initial recognition** of deviant spelling in weak L2 writers? Restricted to spelling because spelling is the error category most dependent on **visual salience** rather than grammatical knowledge.

## Method
- **Design:** Quasi-experimental between-groups study (4 conditions, n = 15 each), administered individually over 4 weekly sessions, **combined with protocol analysis** (written comments + audio-recorded, transcribed think-aloud protocols). One-way ANOVA, **Scheffé** post-hoc, **Omega²**; SPSS; α = 0.05.
- **N:** **60** Spanish native speakers, second-year undergraduate EFL writing students at the University of Granada, **all previously classified as underachieving** on three simultaneous criteria (low band marks 60–71 on three compositions rated by three independent raters; consistent <40% on monthly grammar/reading/listening tests; teacher judgement). Randomly assigned, 15 per condition. **None had received any proofreading instruction** in the course.
- **Materials:** One short continuous text (~172 words) on study habits, judged average difficulty for the cohort. **15 implanted spelling errors — identical in all four versions** — drawn from a database of 130+ examples of *these same students' own* written errors over the previous 9 months, covering 8 error categories: verb/noun confusion, single-for-double consonant, double-for-single consonant, short-for-long vowel, letter reversal, L1 interference, adverb/noun confusion, omission of silent letters. Error positions were redistributed across conditions.
- **Delivery:** text on a computer monitor; a **computer-generated frame highlighted a 4-line section while masking all surrounding lines**, each displayed for **30 seconds** before advancing. Students reported errors orally or by pointing. Students were told not all lines contained errors but that some might contain more than one, to control expectancy.
- **Measures:** BEHAVIORAL — **number of correctly recognized planted errors out of 15**, one point each, **no penalty for false positives**. PROTOCOL — six self-initiated behaviors counted from think-aloud. **No eye-tracking.**

## Style/layout variables manipulated
**Exactly one variable: average line length (characters per line)** — ≈75, ≈60, ≈45, ≈35 cpl. **Font (Times New Roman), font size (14), line spacing (double), print quality, and margins were held constant by design** to control for moderating variables.

## Key findings
- **Mean errors recognized: C1 (≈75 cpl) = 6.33 (SD 0.82); C2 (≈60) = 7.87 (SD 0.99); C3 (≈45) = 8.80 (SD 1.08); C4 (≈35) = 7.67 (SD 1.11).** Grand mean 7.67 (SD 1.32).
- **One-way ANOVA: F = 15.293, p < .001** (printed "0.000"); between df = 3, SS = 46.533, MS = 15.511; within df = 56, SS = 56.800, MS = 1.014.
- **Effect size Omega² = 0.42** — 42% of variance in error recognition is attributable to typographical condition. This is a large and, for this literature, unusually well-quantified effect.
- **Scheffé post-hoc:** C1–C2 = 1.53*; **C1–C3 = 2.47*** (largest; P = .000); C1–C4 = 1.33* (P = .008); C2–C3 = 0.93 n.s.; **C2–C4 = 0.20, P = .960, n.s.**; C3–C4 = 1.13* (P = .031).
- **The effect is an inverted U, not a monotonic improvement:** recognition improves as lines narrow to **≈45 cpl**, then **declines at ≈35 cpl**. The author attributes this to the "mutilation" of text — past a point, narrowing stops helping.
- **Threshold effect on manipulation size:** the 30-character difference C1↔C3 produced a much larger gain than either of the two 15-character differences.
- **Overall detection rate was only ~51%** (grand mean 7.67/15) *despite* the errors being these students' own most frequent, previously flagged errors.
- **Protocol findings (counts only, no statistics):** **21** subjects traced the text with a finger word-by-word; **18** read aloud and **11 more** mouthed or spoke *sotto voce*; **38 sessions** contained moments where the subject sensed something was wrong but could not pinpoint it; **14** subjects could point to the line but not proceed.
- **Typographic recommendation:** ≈**45 cpl is the optimum**, a substantial reduction from ≈75; **do not narrow to ≈35**. The scrolling/masking features of a computer display can divide text into more manageable units.

## Effect direction & size if reported
**Inverted U with a peak at ≈45 cpl.** Omega² = 0.42 for line length. C1 (75 cpl) worst; C3 (45 cpl) best; C4 (35 cpl) significantly worse than C3 (P = .031) and not different from C2 (P = .960).

## Limitations/caveats
- **Only one typographic variable was manipulated.** There is **no evidence in this paper about font type, font size, line spacing, print quality, or margins** — all held constant. Between-word spacing, which the author cites from Lydiatt (1984), is never tested.
- **n = 15 per cell.** The non-monotonic C4 result — which carries the paper's main theoretical claim that "mutilation" stops helping — rests on a single 15-person cell and is a post-hoc interpretation.
- Single text, single 15-error set, one error instance per category; effects may be text- and error-specific.
- **Recognition only.** The second component of the two-component framework (correcting the deviance) is untested here.
- **"Salience" is never measured directly** — no eye-tracking, no perceptual-span measure. It is *inferred* from recognition counts, so the construct-validity link is assumed rather than demonstrated.
- Single common font used to control for student script; the author flags that generalization to students proofreading **their own handwriting** is untested.
- Fixed 30-second-per-section timing simulating exam pressure; between-subject design with no within-subject control; Scheffé is conservative; no effect sizes beyond ω².

## Connections
- **The corpus's strongest single-source evidence that line length is a first-order variable in proofreading** — ω² = 0.42 is far larger than anything else in this literature, and it is the only source that measures it as a *behavioral proofreading outcome* rather than a speed or readability proxy. It should carry weight proportional to that, with the n = 15 caveat attached.
- **The inverted U is the key contribution and it complicates the corpus's line-length consensus.** Nanavati & Bias's recommended 45–75 cpl range stops just short of Porte's ≈35 cpl *decline*, so the two are compatible but not identical — see [[sources/nanavati-bias-2005-optimal-line-length]], [[concepts/line-length]], and the contradictions section of [[syntheses/Proofreading]].
- **Mechanistically the cleanest instance of the perceptual-span account of style effects in this corpus.** The masking frame restricts each visual sweep to 4 lines, so attention lands on surface deviance instead of on 2–3 lexical items for meaning. This is a directly testable prediction linking [[concepts/line-length]] to [[concepts/word-spacing-segmentation]] and to Zola's (1984) 3–5 letter sharp-fixation limit, which Porte cites. Nobody in this corpus has since tested it.
- **The ~51% detection ceiling on the students' own recurring errors is the corpus's best evidence for the proofreading-skill ceiling** that layout can act on but not overcome — see [[concepts/proofreading-skill-instruction]] and [[concepts/error-detection]].
- **The oral-reading and finger-tracing protocol behaviors are the practical recommendation** (slow reading to surface deviance), which belongs alongside the typographic one and is arguably the more transferable finding. No statistical test was applied to any of these counts.

# Nanavati & Bias (2005) — optimal line length in reading (literature review)

## Citation
Nanavati, A. A., & Bias, G. G. (2005). Optimal line length in reading — a literature review. *Visible Language*, 39(2), 120–145.

## Reading task(s)
**comprehension** and **reading speed** (cross-cutting; the review covers both, plus eye movements and visual fatigue).

## Question / aim
Determine the optimal line length for reading, across print and CRT screen displays, by synthesizing effects on reading speed, comprehension, and eye movements.

## Method
- **Design:** **Narrative literature review.** Cites work from 1929 onward (Tinker & Paterson 1929; Luckiesh & Moss 1941; Paterson & Tinker 1942, 1943) across print and screen.
- **N:** **No study count is reported.** No search strategy, databases, keywords, or inclusion/exclusion criteria are stated.
- **Materials:** printed texts and CRT display texts in the reviewed studies; line lengths varied in inches, characters per line, and picas.

## Style/layout variables covered
Line length (cpl / inches / picas); leading as a function of line length; type size; justification; window and screen size; scrolling.

## Key findings
- **Recommended range ≈45–75 characters per line** for most readers; the review notes that historical recommendations vary and that "no significant difference" between 35, 55, 75, and 95 cpl was found for efficiency and satisfaction in at least one study — so the 45–75 figure is a **guideline, not a threshold.**
- **Very short and very long lines both reduce reading efficiency** relative to moderate lengths.
- **Line length interacts with leading:** longer lines need proportionally more leading. The review's practical guidance is leading of **no less than about 1/30 of line length.**
- **Reading distance matters** — optimal line length increases with viewing distance.
- Reviewing comprehension, speed, and eye-movement evidence together, the review notes **short lines can help fast readers** while moderate lengths suit most readers.
- Screen and print differ, citing Duchnicky & Kolers (1983) on window size and Dillon's screen-reading reviews.

## Effect direction & size if reported
**No pooled effect sizes — narrative review, no meta-analysis.** Directional claims only: non-monotonic, with a moderate optimum; the leading × length interaction is the most specific quantitative guidance it offers.

## Limitations/caveats
- **Narrative, not systematic:** no PRISMA, no search strategy, no inclusion criteria, no counted study total, no risk-of-bias appraisal. Treat as a skilled practitioner's synthesis, not a countable evidence base.
- **Predates modern displays entirely.** Its screen evidence concerns CRTs; every screen study in this corpus is 2010s–2020s LCD/e-paper. It cannot adjudicate digital questions.
- **Character counts are typeface-dependent** — 45–75 cpl in one face is a different physical measure in another. This is precisely the measurement problem [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] later formalized by proposing x-height in millimetres instead.
- Optimal length varies by task (skimming vs careful reading), age, font, and screen size — the review says so but does not quantify the interactions.
- **"No significant difference" at 35/55/75/95 cpl sits awkwardly at the boundary of its own recommended range.** The lower bound of the recommendation (45) lies above the shortest condition that showed no difference (35), and the upper bound (75) lies below the longest (95). The range is a compromise, not a boundary.

## Connections
- **The corpus's foundational line-length citation, and the origin of the ≈45–75 cpl figure** that appears throughout the web-legibility literature. Note the tension with [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]], which reports a legible line length of **≈100–120 mm** — a physical measure, not a character count, and not directly comparable without a typeface. [[concepts/line-length]] should hold both and note the units problem rather than choosing.
- **Complements its own caveat about eye movements** with the direct behavioral test in [[sources/porte-2001-typographical-error-salience-l2]], which finds an inverted-U optimum at **≈45 cpl** in proofreading — at the very bottom of Nanavati & Bias's recommended range, and with a *decline* at 35 cpl, just below it. Two sources, one century apart, converging on ~45 as the lower edge of optimal.
- **The 45–75 range is the third distinct unit-based "line length" recommendation in the corpus**, alongside Tarasov's 100–120 mm and the ≈55 cpl web figure. This is concrete support for the measurement-standard argument in [[syntheses/legibility-measurement-standard]].
- Its leading guidance (≥1/30 line length) is the only source in the corpus that states a **leading-to-length ratio**, making it the natural citation for the interaction argument in [[concepts/line-spacing]] and [[concepts/line-length]].
- **The review is notably silent on typography form** — no serif/sans, typeface, or slant content — so it complements rather than duplicates [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] and [[sources/ho-sang-petrarca-2025-beyond-typeface-value]].

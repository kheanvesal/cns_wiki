# Legibility of textbooks: a literature review

## Citation
Tarasov, D. A., Sergeeva, A. P., & Filimonova, V. V. (2015). Legibility of textbooks: a literature review. *Procedia — Social and Behavioral Sciences*, 174, 1300–1308. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sbspro.2015.01.751
Conference: INTE 2014; peer review under responsibility of Sakarya University. Ural Federal University, Dept. of Printing Arts and Web-Design, Yekaterinburg, Russia.

## Reading task(s)
**comprehension** (primary; textbook comprehension and word recognition) · also cross-cutting for **skimming**, **search**, **proofreading** — the variables reviewed (typeface, size, leading, line length, columns) apply to all four.

## Question / aim
To collect the main results of legibility/readability research in order to (a) predict the direction of further research and (b) **identify the factors that induce inconsistencies between findings obtained by different scientists**.

## Method
**Narrative literature review** (not systematic, not a meta-analysis). No new participants or stimuli. Synthesizes ~a century of print legibility work, citing Tinker & Paterson (1928–1950), Paterson & Tinker (1932, 1944, 1946), Cheetham, Arditi & Cho (2005), O'Brien/Mansfield/Legge (2005), Hughes & Wilkins (2000, 2002), Dyson & Kipping (1998), Kruk & Muter (1984), Muter & Maurutto (1991), Spenser (1968), Pardo (2004).
Explicitly notes the literature is **English-centric**: "very few researchers have examined non-English texts," so guidelines "cannot be simply applied to non-English script."

## Style / layout variables manipulated
None manipulated — reviewed: **typeface** (serif vs. sans), **font size** (pt and x-height), **leading / line spacing**, **line length** (mm), **column setting** (1 vs. multicolumn), **paper substrate**, **lighting/viewing conditions** (as methodological variables, not IVs).

## Key findings
- **No consensus on serifs.** "Almost equal numbers of studies showed advantages and disadvantages of serifs."
- **No consensus on columns.** Same even split for number of columns in text.
- **Line length and font size preferences are "highly dispersed."** Reported means: legible line length **≈100–120 mm**; most legible font size **≈12 points — "but without specification of typeface."**
- **No typeface effect on any outcome.** The review "did not reveal any difference between the different typefaces over reading speed, comprehension, and recalling," therefore **"no special type font is suggested to use in print and everyone is free to choose it themselves."** The variables that matter are **subjects' familiarity with the typeface** and **subjects' preferences**.
- **Speed is more typography-sensitive than understanding.** Reading speed, "the main predictor of legibility," is **more sensitive to typographical factors rather than to comprehension and recall**.
- **Font size: an old claim that did not replicate.** Citing Tinker (1963), larger sizes mean peripheral vision perceives fewer words and reading speed *decreases* — "However, many of subsequent studies have found no significant differences between different font sizes."
- **Leading drives the return sweep.** Line spacing has "a great effect on having an easy return sweep"; too little or too much degrades it.
- **Root-cause diagnosis of the field's contradictions.** The review detects "a similar problem in all of studies above. It is an absence of a unified approach… We suggest it is the principal reason of such contradictory results obtained." Named uncontrolled factors: **lighting** (per Daly 1993, lighting affects both color and *qualimetric* perception), **paper/substrate spectral properties**, and **absence of unified measuring units**.
- **Proposed standardization.** Use SI units; **x-height in millimetres as the only measure of a typeface**; express line spacing as a fraction of x-height or in mm; lighting per **ISO 3664:2009**; viewing distance **0.4 m**; report illuminance, spectra, view angle, subjects' visual acuity and glasses; fully specify sheet size, column setting, line length, margins, x-height, mean inter-word/inter-letter/inter-line spacing, full typeface name, and optical density of paper and text.
- Includes a dyslexia-adjacent item: O'Brien, Mansfield & Legge (2005), *the effect of print size on reading speed in dyslexia*.

## Effect direction & size if reported
**No pooled effect sizes — narrative review, no meta-analysis.** Directional claims only: typeface → null on speed, comprehension, recall; serifs → null (evenly split); columns → null (evenly split); line length and font size → highly dispersed with means ~100–120 mm and ~12 pt; reading speed → more typography-sensitive than comprehension/recall; leading → non-monotonic (too little or too much degrades the return sweep).

## Limitations / caveats
- **Narrative, not systematic:** no search strategy, no inclusion/exclusion criteria, no risk-of-bias assessment, no PRISMA. "Almost equal numbers" is a qualitative impression, not a counted, citable proportion — do not treat as a quantified null.
- **Venue:** conference proceedings (INTE 2014, *Procedia-SBS*), not a specialist legibility/typography journal. Weigh accordingly.
- **Print and English only.** Predates the screen/mobile literature entirely; every screen finding in this corpus postdates it, so it cannot adjudicate digital questions.
- **No slant/italic coverage.** The corpus's most convergent style finding — that italics are disadvantaged, especially for dyslexic readers — is **absent** from this review. See [[concepts/italic-and-slant]].
- **No Arnold et al. (2007)** and no contemporary dyslexia typeface work; predates TACCESS 2013/2016 and the 2020s personalization literature.
- Readers are not tracked individually; it aggregates study-level results and so cannot speak to within-person variance (cf. [[sources/accelerating-adult-readers-typeface]]).

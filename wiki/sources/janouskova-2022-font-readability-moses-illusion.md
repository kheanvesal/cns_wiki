# Janoušková et al. (2022) — font readability and the Moses illusion (replication)

## Citation
Janoušková, A., Kocyan, J., Šimová, M., Uvirová, K., Zahradníčková, K., Vaculík, M., & Procházka, J. (2022). The effect of font readability on the Moses illusion: A replication study. *Consciousness and Cognition*, 99, 103284. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2022.103284

## Reading task(s)
**comprehension / error detection** — but **not** continuous-text reading. This is a two-question trivia paradigm, and the wiki's categorization should note that it sits closer to error detection than to reading comprehension.

## Question / aim
Replicate Song & Schwarz (2008): test whether a **difficult-to-read font** increases detection of the Moses illusion error, with mood as a covariate.

## Method
- **Design:** Experimental, **between-subjects**, with an explicit pilot to verify the readability manipulation, plus **equivalence testing (TOST)** with a smallest-effect-of-interest threshold. Proportions compared by z-tests; mood tested by logistic regression.
- **N:** Pilot **16** (comparing Arial vs Allura on a 7-point readability scale: Arial M = 6.125 vs Allura M = 4.625, **t(14) = 2.743, p = .017**). Main study **87** undergraduates (80% women, 19–30 y), randomly assigned to easy (**Arial, n = 43**) or difficult (**Allura, n = 44**).
- **Materials:** **Printed** trivia questions — "Which country is famous for cuckoo clocks, banks, and pocket knives?" (undistorted) and "How many animals of each kind did Moses take on the Ark?" (distorted). Presented in Arial or Allura at the size established by the pilot.

## Style/layout variables manipulated
**Font readability only** — Arial (easy) vs Allura (script/difficult). Single typographic manipulation. Paper texture, size, and layout otherwise constant.

## Key findings
- **The original hypothesis was not supported: font readability did not significantly affect error detection.** Difficult-to-read 43% vs easy-to-read 37% detected the error, **z = −0.57, p = .569**.
- Other response categories null: answered "two" 45% vs 54%, z = 0.75, p = .453; "I don't know" 11% vs 9%, z = −0.32, p = .749.
- **Equivalence testing supports a negligible effect** — TOST was significant against the predefined smallest effect of interest. This is a stronger null than "not significant": the study argues the effect is *small enough to exclude*.
- **The manipulation itself worked** on the undistorted question: correct answers were higher in the easy font (**93% vs 77%, z = 2.06, p = .039**) — so difficult font measurably impaired performance, just not error detection.
- **Mood did not affect error detection** (logistic regression non-significant).

## Effect direction & size if reported
Error detection: **null**, 43% vs 37%, z = −0.57, p = .569, with equivalence established against the smallest effect of interest. Factual answer accuracy: easy font better, 93% vs 77%, z = 2.06, p = .039.

## Limitations/caveats
- **Only two questions, one of them distorted.** Fixed order (undistorted first) could influence detection of the later distorted item.
- **One difficult font only** (Allura). Familiarity with the typeface was not controlled — a significant omission given [[concepts/expertise-familiarity]].
- **The difficult font impaired the control question but not the target behavior.** The manipulation is demonstrably effective on *performance* and demonstrably ineffective on *illusion detection* — which is the interesting result, not a failure of the manipulation.
- Czech population context: the authors note biblical knowledge may be less actively used, though knowledge appeared present.
- Questions were **not normed.** Motivation/warning given only at the beginning.
- **Paradigm mismatch:** a two-item trivia task is not continuous-text reading, so this should not be pooled with reading-comprehension studies despite landing in the comprehension folder.

## Connections
- **A direct failed replication of the disfluency account, and one of the few sources in this corpus to use equivalence testing** — which converts "not significant" into "shown to be negligible." That methodological move is worth citing in [[concepts/methodology-critique]] as the standard the field rarely meets.
- **The most useful thing this source does for the disfluency literature is dissociate disfluency's effects.** Difficult font → *worse factual accuracy* (p = .039) but *no change in error detection* (p = .569). So disfluency costs performance without necessarily promoting the reflective, error-catching state that the desirable-difficulty account predicts. This is a genuine mechanistic problem for [[concepts/disfluency]] and [[concepts/desirable-difficulty]], and it should be recorded there as a counterweight to the usual "disfluent fonts improve memory" claim.
- **Also relevant to the font-recommendation question:** a hard-to-read script face did not produce a benefit even in the paradigm (an illusion) where Song & Schwarz reported one. That weakens any general claim that legibility difficulty is protective. Compare [[concepts/sans-forgetica]], where the same hypothesis failed to replicate in continuous text.
- **Familiarity left uncontrolled** makes this a clean example of the gap [[concepts/expertise-familiarity]] documents — a single unfamiliar face is being compared against a familiar one (Arial).

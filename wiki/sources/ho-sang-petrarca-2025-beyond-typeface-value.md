# Ho Sang & Petrarca (2025) — beyond (type)face value, systematic review

## Citation
Ho Sang, B., & Petrarca, D. (2025). Beyond (type)face value: a systematic literature review examining design factors influencing the legibility and readability of typography. *Visible Language*, 59(3), 262–294.

## Reading task(s)
**cross-cutting — all four reading tasks.** The review covers Latin-alphabet typeface design and typesetting effects on reading words (explicitly excluding isolated-character identification) in print and on screen.

## Question / aim
What design factors most influence the legibility and readability of accessible typography, focusing on typeface design and typesetting for Latin-alphabet languages?

## Method
- **Design:** **Systematic literature review** (PRISMA-like). Search: (typograph* OR typeface OR font) AND (legibility OR readability OR accessibility OR "reading speed"). Databases: Education Source (EBSCOhost), ERIC (ProQuest), APA PsycInfo (ProQuest), Web of Science; **first 100 Google Scholar hits**; handsearch of references. Eligibility: peer-reviewed empirical (qualitative/quantitative/mixed), English, **2000–2025**, typeface/typographic variables, Latin alphabet, print or on-screen reading. **Excluded: specialist typefaces, isolated character identification, some age ranges.**
- **N:** **42 peer-reviewed empirical studies** (30 quantitative, 12 mixed); **3,323 total participants**; 10 countries; 36 in English. **Disabilities only 7% (n = 238):** 3 dyslexia studies (n = 53), 4 low-vision (n = 40), 1 unspecified (n = 145).
- **Materials:** print and on-screen texts; 7 studies used custom typefaces; **52 typefaces** in total (Times/Times New Roman n = 14, Arial n = 11, Georgia n = 7, Verdana n = 6).

## Style/layout variables covered
Typeface design — serifs (n = 11), letter structure (n = 3), letter width (n = 2), stroke contrast, typeface variation. Typographic — **type size (n = 18)**, letter spacing/tracking (n = 6), **line length (n = 9)**, **line spacing/leading (n = 8)**, paragraph spacing (n = 4), letter case (n = 4), color (n = 2), columns (n = 1), text alignment (n = 1), whitespace/layout.

## Measures in included studies
EYE-TRACKING n = 9. BEHAVIORAL — reading speed n = 24, reading accuracy n = 11, reading time/glance n = 2, task completion time n = 1, test scores n = 14, reading acuity n = 1, type size threshold n = 4. SELF-REPORT — questionnaires/ratings n = 12.

## Key findings
- **Serifs are not a significant legibility factor on their own.** Roughly half of serif-vs-sans studies found a null or inconclusive effect; some found conditional benefits in both directions. **No study showed serifs alone caused a significant performance difference.** Includes a null for low vision (Arditi & Cho 2005).
- **No single type size optimizes readability for everyone.** Reported optima vary (Sheedy et al. 2005 ≈10 pt; Sieghart 2023 ≈12 pt) and depend on typeface, x-height, and reading context — so **point sizes are not comparable across typefaces.**
- **Familiarity appears influential but is inconsistently supported:** it improves performance in some studies (Slattery & Rayner 2010; Ukonu et al. 2021) and is null in others. Readers can also **adapt** to different legibility levels (Minakata & Beier 2021).
- **Typeface effects are mixed, but sans serif is often preferred subjectively** (Banerjee et al. 2011; Krivec et al. 2020; Ukonu et al. 2021; Wallace et al. 2022) — and preference is **not reliably linked to performance.**
- **Stroke contrast effects reverse by reader:** for normal vision, best with **serif + high stroke contrast** and **sans + low stroke contrast**; **low vision shows the opposite pattern** (Minakata & Beier 2022; Minakata et al. 2023).
- **Letter width variations did not significantly affect reading speed** (Minakata & Beier 2021; Gasser et al. 2005).
- **Bold can increase reaction times in word alternation** (Dyson & Beier 2016). **Italics can impair reading speed or have a null effect** (Slattery & Rayner 2010).
- **Uppercase can be read faster at smaller sizes and is more readable for low vision, but similar at larger sizes** (Arditi & Cho 2007).
- **Spacing effects are mixed**: crowding is moderated by spacing, with negative effects from both decreased *and* excessive spacing (Beier & Oderkerk 2019; Liu & Arditi 2001; Risko et al. 2011; Schneps et al. 2013; Slattery & Rayner 2013).
- **Line length: medium lengths often read faster than shorter ones** in some studies, with preferences varying (Dyson & Haselgrove 2001; Schneps et al. 2013).

## Effect direction & size if reported
**No pooled effect sizes — a review of directions and counts, not a meta-analysis.** The most quantifiable claim is the **serif null**: ~half of the 11 serif studies found no effect or were inconclusive, and none found a significant serif-only advantage.

## Limitations/caveats
- **Word-identification tasks limit ecological validity** — continuous text is a different task.
- 7 studies use **custom typefaces** to isolate variables, which are not always commercially available.
- **Point-size comparisons across typefaces are problematic** — the review itself flags x-height/visual angle as better (the same critique [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] makes formally).
- Heterogeneous measures and tasks across included studies. Personalized-typography solutions depend on ICT access — a **digital-divide** caveat.
- **Only 7% of the 3,323 participants had any disability**, and dyslexia studies total just 53 participants — so the corpus's most-studied population is thinly represented even in this large review.
- **No formal risk-of-bias instrument is applied per study**; quality appraisal is descriptive.
- English-dominant (36 of 42), which limits the cross-script generalization that [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] also flags.

## Connections
- **The most current and most rigorous typography synthesis in the corpus**, and the strongest support for the corpus-wide typeface null. Where [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] (2015, narrative review, print only) says "no special typeface," this review (2025, 42 studies, systematic, print + screen) says the same with a countable base: **~half of serif studies null, none significant.**
- **Its familiarity finding is the pivot that resolves a long-standing contradiction.** [[concepts/expertise-familiarity]] currently holds Tarasov's claim (familiarity and preference decide) against [[sources/accelerating-adult-readers-typeface]]'s direct test (familiarity predicts nothing: r = .042 speed, r = .033 comprehension). Ho Sang & Petrarca land on the *same* mixed verdict — influential in some studies, null in others, with adaptation also documented — which makes the contradiction look like a **genuine unresolved question rather than one side being wrong**. Update the concept page to say so.
- **The stroke-contrast reversal by reader is a clean interaction finding with no counterpart elsewhere in the corpus.** It is the best available evidence that typeface parameters must be specified *per reader group*, which is what [[concepts/individual-differences-in-readability]] argues in general terms. See also the cross-language non-replication in [[concepts/dyslexia-font-recommendations]].
- **Its italic finding (impair or null, depending on study) sits against the corpus's convergent italic penalty** at [[concepts/italic-and-slant]] — where the penalty appears in *tracking metrics* specifically. Read together: italic is reliably worse as measured by fixations, and equivocal as measured by speed.
- **Low-vision findings (uppercase better at small sizes; stroke-contrast pattern reversed) point away from dyslexia recommendations toward low vision**, a population almost absent from the rest of the corpus. A genuine gap.
- **Its x-height caution is independent methodological support for [[syntheses/legibility-measurement-standard]]** — two reviews, arriving at the same measurement critique a decade apart.

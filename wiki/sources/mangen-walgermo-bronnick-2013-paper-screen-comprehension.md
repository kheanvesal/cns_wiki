# Mangen, Walgermo & Brønnick (2013) — paper vs screen, linear texts

## Citation
Mangen, A., Walgermo, B. R., & Brønnick, K. (2013). Reading linear texts on paper versus computer screen: Effects on reading comprehension. *International Journal of Educational Research*, 58, 61–68.

## Reading task(s)
**comprehension** (primary). Text genre (narrative vs expository) is a secondary factor.

## Question / aim
How do **linear, sequential** texts — as opposed to hypertext — differ in comprehension when read on screen versus on paper? Motivated by the Norwegian school practice of delivering geography, science, and history as PDFs alongside textbooks.

## Method
- **Design:** **Between-groups randomized experiment**, sequential (blockwise) regression, with multicollinearity checked via Belsley, Kuh & Welsch (1980). SPSS 18.03.
- **N:** **72** tenth graders from two primary schools in Norway. **Paper n = 25; computer n = 47** (unbalanced). Pretest equivalence confirmed: vocabulary t(70) = .41, p = .88; word-chain t(70) = .25, p = .80.
- **Materials:** Two texts of **1,400–2,000 words** — one narrative, one expository — school subject matter. Digital version presented as **PDF in Adobe Reader 9.4**, chosen specifically to minimize scrolling. Print: A4, black **14-pt Times New Roman** at 100% scale. **1-hour time limit**; all students finished inside it. All scales α > .75.
- **Measures:** BEHAVIORAL only — comprehension test score, narrative-vs-expository standardized difference score, vocabulary and word-reading pretests. **No eye-tracking, no self-report, no reading-speed measure.**

## Style/layout variables manipulated
Reading medium (A4 print vs PDF on 15" LCD); text genre; typeface held constant at 14-pt Times New Roman across conditions.

## Key findings
- **Paper significantly outperformed screen on comprehension:** standardized **b = −.216, p = .025** (Table 2 prints b = −.21), indicating digitally-reading students were more likely to score lower.
- Adding modality raised explained variance by **ΔR² = .04, F = 5.24, p = .025**; final model **R² = .42**.
- **Genre did not interact with modality:** F(1,70) = .142, **p = .707**. The prediction that screen would hurt expository text more than narrative was **not supported**.
- Pretest balance was tight and slightly *favored* the computer group on comprehension (M = 19.7, SD 8.2 vs paper M = 19.5, SD 6.3; effect size −.02).
- The authors' proposed mechanism is **navigation/spatial instability**, but they concede they have "no data showing whether or not, and to what extent" students scrolled.

## Effect direction & size if reported
Comprehension: paper > screen, b = −.216, p = .025, ΔR² = .04. Genre moderation: null, p = .707. Reading speed: **not measured**.

## Limitations/caveats
- **Both groups answered the comprehension questions on screen** — only the *encoding* medium varied while the *test* medium was digital in both arms. A serious confound given that the outcome is the comprehension score itself.
- **Reading speed was never measured**, so this study cannot speak to the speed side of the screen penalty.
- Unbalanced groups (25 vs 47). Single national context. Scrolling permitted but unmeasured and unexcluded.
- Unnaturalistic condition match: 14-pt Times New Roman on a 15" 1280×1024 LCD vs A4 print.
- **Internal reporting errors:** the text claims the model was "significantly improved by each step," but Step 2 (adding sex) was **p = .121**; prose coefficients for word reading (b = .144) and vocabulary (b = .551) contradict Table 2's final model (.24 and .27) by margins too large for rounding; and the ΔR² values sum to .41 while Step 3 reports R² = .42.

## Connections
- **A likely load-bearing study for the pooled estimate in [[sources/kong-seo-zhai-2018-screen-paper-meta-analysis]]** — `b = −.216` there sits almost exactly on the pooled `g = −.21`. But Kong's non-significant screen-type moderator (p = .41) means this computer-screen result is pooled with tablet/e-reader conditions it does not represent.
- The nearest **primary-school** evidence in the corpus; the screen-inferiority effect in this sample is the one Kong's K–12 subset (only 4 of 17 studies) most directly reflects.
- Its genre-interaction null (p = .707) is the key counterweight to text-difficulty accounts of screen inferiority — see [[concepts/screen-vs-paper]] and the shallow-vs-deep split in [[sources/chen-et-al-2014-paper-screen-tablet-familiarity]] (deep comprehension null, p = .169).
- Directly contradicts the "digital is faster" position implied by the (non-significant) pooled speed point estimate in Kong; speed was never measured here, so there is no direct conflict.

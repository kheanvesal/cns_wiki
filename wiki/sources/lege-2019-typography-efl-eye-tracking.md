# Lege (2019) — typography's effect on L2 learners' reading processes

## Citation
Lege, R. (2019). Typography's effect on language learners' reading processes: an eye tracking study. *The Asian EFL Journal*, Issue 21, Volume 3. **No DOI provided.**

## Reading task(s)
**comprehension** and **recall** in L2/EFL reading, with eye-tracking as the process measure.

## Question / aim
Investigate the effects of **typographic cueing and hierarchy** on reading patterns, recall, and comprehension of L2 learners. Three research questions: (1) does cueing increase comprehension/recall? (2) which cues are most effective? (3) how do cues influence access to content?

## Method
- **Design:** Experimental, **between-subjects**, random assignment. Eye-tracking plus post-reading comprehension/recall (multiple-choice + open-ended).
- **N:** **65 initially; 45 with valid eye-tracking data** (23 designed, 22 undesigned) after tracking loss. Japanese university EFL majors, TOEIC ≥480, ages 19–21, no long-term residence abroad.
- **Materials:** A **140-word** passage on phonology in Helvetica. Control = "undersigned" (Helvetica 12 pt, consistent); experimental = "designed" — the same base text with **21 typographic changes** creating hierarchy and cueing.
- **Apparatus:** **Eye Tribe, 30–60 Hz.**
- **Measures:** EYE-TRACKING — fixations, **percent fixated per AOI**, reading patterns/heatmaps. BEHAVIORAL — total comprehension/recall score, MCQ score, open-ended score.

## Style/layout variables manipulated
**Typographic hierarchy** (headings/subheadings size); **typographic cues** — **bold, italic, color, baseline shift, size adjustments** relative to a 12 pt base. Layout held at line length 157 mm, leading 20 pt.

## Key findings
- **Designed group scored significantly higher on total posttest:** M = 8.78 vs 5.68, **t = 3.532, p < .001** (ANOVA p < .001).
- **MCQ scores higher for designed:** M = 2.83 vs 1.82, **t = 3.57, p < .001**.
- **Open-ended recall higher for designed:** M = 5.96 vs 3.86, **t = 2.62, p = .012**.
- **Average fixations and overall percent fixated were NOT significantly different** between groups. Percent fixated was numerically higher for designed (67.38% vs 64.66%), null.
- **One AOI distinguished the groups sharply:** AOI 6 was fixated by **100%** of the designed group vs **62.07%** of the undesigned, **t = 3.273, p = 0.0021**. AOI 6 carried **multiple cues** (size, baseline shift, bold).
- Several AOIs (1, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 21) were fixated >10% more in the designed condition, though not all reached significance; others were higher in the undesigned condition.
- **Reading patterns differed qualitatively:** the designed group focused on headings and large words (multiple access points); the undesigned followed a more linear top-down pattern.
- The significant AOI differences were concentrated in regions with **size and baseline shift** — i.e. the cues that move attention most.

## Effect direction & size if reported
Typographic cueing + hierarchy → **substantially higher comprehension and recall** (total posttest p < .001; MCQ p < .001; open-ended p = .012) with **no change in overall fixation quantity**, but a **redistribution** of attention toward cued regions (AOI 6: 100% vs 62.07%, p = .0021).

## Limitations/caveats
- **Immediate posttest only** — no retention measure, so the effects may be short-lived.
- **Cues were bundled.** Twenty-one changes were applied together; **no single cue type was isolated**, so the study cannot say which cue did the work, or whether there is an optimal cue count. This is the same confound the italic evidence suffers from, at a much larger scale.
- **Small sample after tracking loss** (N = 45), and the loss itself is unexplained.
- **Japanese L2 learners only** — limits generalization, and the L2 population may be especially sensitive to structural cues precisely because they have weaker language control. That's a plausible confound for a hierarchy/cueing effect: it may reflect reduced reading automaticity rather than a general typography principle.
- 140-word passage; short; limited ecological validity.
- **Eye Tribe at 30–60 Hz** is low-resolution by current standards, which affects AOI-level fixation counts specifically.

## Connections
- **Strongest single-study support in the corpus for typographic hierarchy as a comprehension intervention**, and the only one with an eye-tracking account of *why* it works: attention is redistributed toward cued regions rather than simply increased. That distinction matters for [[concepts/selective-attention]] and [[concepts/signaling]].
- **Directly relevant to the headings evidence already in the corpus.** [[sources/lemarie-lorch-perywoodley-2012-headings]] and [[sources/functional-headings-selective-attention]] support headings via *selective attention*; [[sources/scaltritti-2019-typographic-variables-webpage-eye-movements]] found more headers → fewer fixations (b = −0.92), interpreted as reduced second-pass reading. Lege adds the outcome side (better recall) that those correlational sources lack. See [[concepts/headings-signaling]].
- **The cue-bundling confound is this source's most important limitation and should be named explicitly** in [[concepts/signaling]] — 21 simultaneous changes cannot establish which cue matters, and "typographic cueing" should not be cited as a single manipulable factor.
- **The null on total fixations with a significant effect on recall** is another instance of the corpus's recurring pattern: eye-movement quantities are not the mechanism, redistribution is. Compare [[sources/chinese-typography-eye-tracking-vertical-horizontal]], where the significant measure was pupil diameter rather than fixation count.
- **Its L2 sample is a double-edged contribution:** it extends typography effects to language learners, but also means the strong hierarchy effect may be specific to readers with weaker automaticity. Relevant to [[concepts/individual-differences-in-readability]].

# Signaling

**What.** Cues (headings, highlighting, connectives) that mark structure/importance without adding content; a multimedia-learning principle.

**Sources.** [[sources/lemarie-lorch-perywoodley-2012-headings]] · [[sources/mayer-fiorella-intro-multimedia-learning]] · [[sources/lege-2019-typography-efl-eye-tracking]]

## The best outcome evidence, and the confound that limits it
[[sources/lege-2019-typography-efl-eye-tracking]] is the corpus's strongest single-study support for typographic hierarchy as a **comprehension and recall** intervention, and the only source that shows *how* it works:

- Designed text (21 typographic changes creating hierarchy) beat undesigned on total posttest (M = 8.78 vs 5.68, t = 3.532, p < .001), on multiple-choice (2.83 vs 1.82, p < .001), and on open-ended recall (5.96 vs 3.86, p = .012).
- **Average fixations and overall percent fixated did not differ.** The significant eye-tracking result was **redistribution**: AOI 6 — the region carrying multiple cues — was fixated by **100%** of the designed group vs **62.07%** of the undesigned (t = 3.273, p = .0021). Significant AOI differences concentrated in the regions with **size and baseline shift**.
- Qualitatively, the designed group jumped to headings and large words (multiple access points); the undesigned group followed a more linear top-down path.

**Read this as "attention is redirected, not recruited."** Total attentional budget unchanged; its allocation changed. That is a more precise claim than "cues make readers read better," and it fits [[concepts/selective-attention]] and [[concepts/resource-rationality]].

## ⚠️ The bundled-cue problem
Lege's design applies **21 typographic changes simultaneously** — heading size, subheading size, bold, italic, colour, baseline shift, size adjustments — against a plain 12 pt Helvetica control. **No single cue type was isolated.** Lege can therefore support "structured typographic hierarchy beats flat text" and cannot support "bold helps," "colour helps," or any claim about a cue count. **Do not cite this source for any individual cue.**

This is a corpus-wide weakness, not a one-off. [[sources/porte-2001-typographical-error-salience-l2]] is the methodological counter-example — it manipulated line length *alone* with font, size, spacing, print quality and margins all fixed, and can therefore carry a causal weight Lege's cannot. The contrast is instructive: **the corpus's largest reported cue effect comes from the study with the weakest internal validity, and its cleanest causal claim comes from the study with the smallest effect size.**

Also unresolved: the Lege effect may be **specific to L2 readers with weaker automaticity**, who may lean harder on structural cues. The sample was Japanese EFL majors (N = 45 after tracking loss, Eye Tribe at 30–60 Hz), so the effect's generality to fluent readers is untested.

## Relation to the headings evidence
- [[sources/lemarie-lorch-perywoodley-2012-headings]] and [[sources/functional-headings-selective-attention]] support headings via *selective attention*.
- [[sources/scaltritti-2019-typographic-variables-webpage-eye-movements]] found **more headers → fewer fixations (b = −0.92)**, read as reduced second-pass reading — the same redirection, observed correlationally.
- Lege adds the **outcome side** (better recall) that the correlational sources lack. Together these are the corpus's best-supported signaling story: cues redirect attention toward structural regions, which supports recall.

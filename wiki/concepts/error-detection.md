# Error Detection

**What it is.** The core proofreading outcome — spotting typos, misspellings, and deviations. Measured as detection rate and time. **Recognition, not correction**: the corpus contains no study that separates the two components cleanly.

**Key patterns.**
- Line spacing/number significantly affect detection rate and time [[sources/chan-2014-line-length-spacing-proofreading]].
- Text features determine how errors pop out vs. get missed [[sources/mouthaan-vitello-2022-text-feature-effects]]; medium (screen vs paper) modulates detection [[sources/vitello-2022-screen-vs-paper]].
- Detection depends on slow, surface-level attention — different from meaning-building (see [[syntheses/Proofreading]]).

## The strongest layout effect on detection in the corpus
[[sources/porte-2001-typographical-error-salience-l2]] is the only source that manipulates a typographic variable alone and measures recognition directly. Errors came from the participants' **own** most frequent spelling errors over the preceding 9 months (15 implanted, 8 categories), so no error was unfamiliar:

- **Line length alone: ω² = .42**, F = 15.293, p < .001 — **42% of the variance in error recognition** attributable to typographical condition, with font, size, spacing, print quality and margins all held constant.
- **Inverted U.** Recognition rose from ≈75 cpl (M = 6.33/15) to ≈60 (7.87) to a **peak at ≈45 (8.80)**, then **fell at ≈35 (7.67)**. The C3–C4 decline is significant (P = .031). Porte's "mutilation" reading of this is post-hoc on one 15-person cell.

**Why this is the corpus's best mechanism story.** The display masked everything outside a highlighted 4-line frame. That restricts each visual sweep to four lines, so attention lands on surface deviance rather than on 2–3 lexical items for meaning — a direct instantiation of the perceptual-span account in [[concepts/proofreading-skill-instruction]] and Zola's 3–5 letter sharp-fixation limit, which Porte cites. **No one in this corpus has tested this prediction since 2001.**

## The skill ceiling that layout cannot overcome
Porte's **grand mean was 7.67 of 15 — a ~51% detection rate** — on errors these students had personally made, been flagged for, and repeated for nine months, while reading under fixed 30-second-per-section pressure. Read against a **.42** variance share, this is the corpus's sharpest statement of the two-part picture: layout moves detection a great deal, and it still leaves roughly half of the participants' own recurring errors undetected. The transferable part of Porte's advice is behavioral rather than typographic — 21 subjects finger-traced word by word, 18 read aloud, 11 more mouthed text *sotto voce*, and in 38 sessions participants **felt something was wrong without being able to locate it** (counts only; no statistics were applied).

## A case where the cost appeared without the benefit
[[sources/janouskova-2022-font-readability-moses-illusion]] shows that harder-to-read text is not a route to better error catching. The difficult font **lowered factual accuracy** (93% vs 77%, z = 2.06, p = .039) while **leaving illusion-error detection unchanged** (43% vs 37%, z = −0.57, p = .569). The manipulation demonstrably worked; the reflective outcome it was supposed to improve did not move. This is a failed replication of Song & Schwarz (2008) **in the paradigm where the original effect was found**, and — unusually for this literature — it was established with **equivalence testing** against a pre-specified smallest-effect threshold, so the null is a demonstrated *negligibility*, not a failure to reject.

Note the paradigm difference: Janoušková's errors are **semantic/knowledge-based** (the Moses illusion), Porte's are **surface orthographic**. The two together suggest that typography may aid surface deviance detection while doing nothing for meaning-based error catching. No study in the corpus crosses the two error types.

## Unmeasured construct
**Salience is never measured directly anywhere in this corpus.** Porte infers it from recognition counts with no eye-tracking; Janoušková infers it from a two-item trivia task. So the word "salience" in this literature is a *label applied to an inference from accuracy*, not an observation. See [[concepts/methodology-critique]].

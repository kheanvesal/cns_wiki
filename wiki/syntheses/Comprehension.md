# Comprehension — Reading-Task Hub

How text style, layout, and typography affect *deep understanding* (building a coherent [[concepts/situation-model]], not just decoding). Theory anchor: [[sources/kintsch-1998-comprehension]] (Construction–Integration; surface → textbase → situation model). Baseline: deeper comprehension levels are rarer than literal ([[sources/fpsyg-12-712901]]), so style factors that free cognitive resources matter most for depth.

## What helps
- **Adequate font size.** 12pt read faster than 10pt; but the benefit is on *speed/legibility*, not comprehension itself [[sources/ej1079769]].
- **Moderate line length / characters-per-line.** cpl is the critical layout mediator on screen [[sources/layout-on-screen]].
- **Structure-aligned formatting.** Graphic organizers matched to text structure aid comprehension + summarization (T-Chart > Venn, d=0.56) [[sources/ej1486502]]; meaningful segmentation + indentation helps [[sources/ssrn-5678368]]; concept-map structure aids EFL recall [[sources/18_63]].
- **Managing text–graphic integration.** Reducing split-attention/cuing helps, especially lower-ability readers [[sources/3320435-3320447]].
- **Visual line breaks aligned to the structure being reconstructed** — the one clean causal layout result: metrical anomalies disrupted reading most in *poem* layout while rhyme anomalies disrupted it most in *prose* layout, so line breaks are cues whose utility depends on which structure the reader must rebuild [[sources/rhythmic-subvocalization-poetry-eye-tracking]]. See [[concepts/layout-and-line-breaks]].
- **Objective per-reader calibration (typography).** Personalized font/size/spacing selected by measured reading performance beat standard defaults within-child: errors 2.69 → 1.63 (~39% reduction, `p=.028`) across 78 Italian children [[sources/validating-personalized-visual-auditory-parameters-dyslexia]]. The speed gain was marginal with identical medians — treat as an *accuracy* result. See [[concepts/individual-differences-in-readability]].
- **Slowed, lower-pitched TTS as support, for readers with reading disorders** — rate 0.8, pitch 0.6; the personalization benefit was concentrated in the atypical group for dictation (`Z=2.05, p=.020`) [[sources/validating-personalized-visual-auditory-parameters-dyslexia]]. See [[concepts/multimodal-and-audio-support]].
- **Pragmatic literacy adaptation (simulation evidence).** Adaptive reading significantly improved outcomes in a computer-science domain [[sources/adaptive-personalization-educational-readings-simulated]] — but see Contradictions: it was not domain-general.

## What is neutral or null
- **Line spacing & letter spacing:** no significant comprehension effect in print [[sources/ej1079769]] or on mobile [[sources/interletter-line-spacing-reading-speed-comprehension]] (though comfort differs).
- **Font type/typeface:** no comprehension effect in EFL print [[sources/ej1079769]]; disfluency effects contested [[sources/ssrn-5678368]].
- **Comprehension under context-adaptive typography:** SituFont improved reading goodput and reduced NASA-TLX workload, but **comprehension accuracy was flat** [[sources/situfont-adaptive-mobile-typography-svi]]. Adaptation buys efficiency and effort reduction, not understanding.
- **Font familiarity:** predicts neither reading speed (`r=0.042`) nor comprehension (`r=0.033`) [[sources/accelerating-adult-readers-typeface]]. **Now tested more thoroughly and still null:** [[sources/ho-sang-petrarca-2025-beyond-typeface-value]] found familiarity did **not** predict recall (null across three experiments) and produced no speed advantage; the one positive was r = .152, p = .041 in a 17-reader pilot. See [[concepts/expertise-familiarity]].
- **Italic in dyslexia:** numerically slower than roman in every contrast but **not significant** (`W=4556, p=.09`) [[sources/good-fonts-for-dyslexia]]; significant within the 2016 revision overall (`χ²=27.27, p<.001`) but **not within the dyslexia group** (`p=.120`) [[sources/font-type-screen-readability-dyslexia]]. See [[concepts/italic-and-slant]].
- **Text color:** only exploratory evidence; no comprehension data anywhere in the corpus [[concepts/text-color-and-visual-salience]].
- **Medium, in first graders:** [[sources/florit-2025-first-grade-comprehension-monitoring]] (N = 58, mean age 6.8) found **no medium main effect** on main-point, literal or inferential comprehension (all ps > .15) at constant font size, and **tablet > paper** for main-point questions on descriptive texts — the opposite of the pooled direction. See the contradictions section.
- **Dyslexia-friendly typography, for dyslexia specifically:** [[sources/arya-2023-assessment-methods-readability-legibility]] reports that increased spacing and dyslexia-friendly fonts significantly improve readability for readers with visual impairments and dyslexia — which sits in tension with [[concepts/dyslexia-font-recommendations]]'s finding that recommended *font sets* do not replicate across languages. The available reconciliation is that **spacing helps where typeface identity does not**. See [[concepts/letter-spacing]].

## ★ Typographic hierarchy: the strongest outcome-side result, with a bundled-cue caveat
[[sources/lege-2019-typography-efl-eye-tracking]] gives this hub its best *comprehension and recall* evidence for structure-aligned formatting. A "designed" version of a 140-word passage (21 typographic changes creating hierarchy) beat a plain Helvetica control on total posttest (**M = 8.78 vs 5.68, p < .001**), multiple choice (2.83 vs 1.82, p < .001) and open recall (5.96 vs 3.86, p = .012).

- **The mechanism is redirection, not recruitment.** Average fixations and overall percent fixated did **not** differ; the significant eye-tracking result was redistribution toward cued regions (**AOI 6: 100% vs 62.07%, p = .0021**), concentrating in the regions with size and baseline shift.
- **⚠️ 21 simultaneous changes means no individual cue is identified.** Cite this for "structured hierarchy beats flat text," never for bold, colour, or italic alone. See [[concepts/signaling]].
- Sample was 45 Japanese L2 learners after tracking loss, Eye Tribe at 30–60 Hz, 140 words, immediate posttest only — so the effect may be specific to readers with weaker automaticity and may not persist.

## Moderators (the interactions are the point)
- **★ Reading medium — the corpus's largest cluster, and it is internally divided.** Pooled: comprehension favors paper at **g = −.21, 95% CI [−.38, −.03], p = .02** (17 studies), while **speed does not differ** (g = .48, p = .11) [[sources/kong-seo-zhai-2018-screen-paper-meta-analysis]], corroborating [[sources/clinton-2019-paper-vs-screens-meta]]. Primary studies reproduce the dissociation: comprehension d = .41, p = .036 with speed p = .729 [[sources/halamish-elbaz-2020-childrens-comprehension-metacomprehension]]. **But no moderator in the meta-analysis reaches significance** — not year, country, or screen type. See [[concepts/screen-vs-paper]] for the five open disputes.
- **Reading purpose/goal:** study vs. entertainment changes *online* inferential activity but not always *offline* comprehension scores [[sources/journal-of-educational-psychology-1999]]; comprehension is a situated, goal-driven decision [[sources/britt-et-al-dp-2022-r1]].
- **Reader ability / expertise:** modulates gaze and susceptibility to split-attention [[sources/3320435-3320447]]; identifier-style advantage shifts with training [[sources/binkley-identifier-style-effort-comprehension-2012]].
- **Modality:** print vs. screen vs. mobile change which layout variables bind [[sources/layout-on-screen]].
- **Who the reader is — the strongest moderator in this batch.** A 51% within-person WPM spread between a reader's own fastest and slowest font, with no font best for anyone [[sources/accelerating-adult-readers-typeface]]; 11 distinct fonts selected as optimal by 49 atypical readers with none exceeding ~20% [[sources/validating-personalized-visual-auditory-parameters-dyslexia]]. Within-person variance exceeds between-font variance.
- **Dyslexia status changes *which* variable matters.** Group membership predicted **font size** (Somers' `D=0.273, p=.013`) but **not** spacing (`p=.436`) and **not** font choice (`p>.05`) [[sources/validating-personalized-visual-auditory-parameters-dyslexia]] — atypical readers dispersed across larger sizes and chose widest letter spacing (40.8% at level 4 vs 34.5% of typical readers at level 1).
- **Content type × layout:** prosody and line-break alignment interact (see What helps) [[sources/rhythmic-subvocalization-poetry-eye-tracking]].
- **Reading direction (script-specific):** vertical Chinese text drew more attention and better recall than horizontal (`t=3.569, p=0.001`) [[sources/chinese-typography-eye-tracking-vertical-horizontal]] — a moderator that has no analog in Latin-script work. See [[concepts/text-direction]].
- **Language/script:** the dyslexia typeface evidence does not transfer across languages — English (Helvetica/CMU/Arial), Turkish (BonvenoCF/TTKB), and Italian (no winner) all disagree. See [[concepts/dyslexia-font-recommendations]].
- **Measurement instrument:** EOG vs. video eye tracking changes what can be claimed about *why* a text was hard. See [[concepts/eog-and-physiological-signals]] and [[syntheses/when-measures-disagree]].
- **Which outcome is measured decides whether style appears to work at all.** The corpus's strongest general law: reading speed is "more sensitive to typographical factors rather than to comprehension and recall" [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]]. A typography null on comprehension is therefore usually a null on the *least* typography-sensitive outcome — especially given that legibility itself has been operationalized as speed (after Hughes & Wilkins). See [[concepts/method-type-divergence]].
- **Unit and medium of the comparison:** line length ≈100–120 mm (print review) vs. ~55 cpl (screen) vs. 45–75 chars (craft norm) are **not the same quantity**, because physical width depends on typeface and type size [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] · [[concepts/line-length]] · [[concepts/methodology-critique]]. The corpus now holds a three-way version of this problem: the 45–75 cpl range originates in [[sources/nanavati-bias-2005-optimal-line-length]], whose own tested condition showed **no significant difference at 35/55/75/95 cpl** — so its own bounds sit inside a null.
- **Screen type, not "screen":** within a single study, **paper beat computer (p = .004) but not tablet (p = .214)** [[sources/chen-et-al-2014-paper-screen-tablet-familiarity]]. Averaging these is what produces the pooled screen-type null (b = .22, p = .41).
- **Comprehension depth:** the medium effect appeared on **shallow** (multiple-choice) comprehension but **not on deep** comprehension (summarization, F = 1.81, p = .169); **device familiarity** affected *deep* comprehension only (F = 5.89, p = .008) [[sources/chen-et-al-2014-paper-screen-tablet-familiarity]]. This is the corpus's only depth-resolved test of medium, and it argues against blanket screen-inferiority. See [[concepts/comprehension-levels]].

## Contradictions / open tensions
- **★ Root cause, offered by the corpus itself.** [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] reviews ~a century of print legibility work and diagnoses the field's contradictions as a **methodological** failure, not a substantive one: "It is an absence of a unified approach… We suggest it is the principal reason of such contradictory results obtained." Named causes: uncontrolled lighting, uncontrolled paper substrate, and **incompatible measurement units**. Its proposal — **x-height in millimetres as the only typeface measure**, leading as a fraction of x-height, ISO 3664:2009 lighting, 0.4 m viewing distance — was never adopted by any study in this corpus. **Read the typeface/serif disagreements below as possibly unanswerable with the instruments the field actually used**, rather than as open questions awaiting one more study. See [[concepts/methodology-critique]].
- **★ The typeface null, stated over the whole literature.** The same review found "almost equal numbers of studies showed advantages and disadvantages of serifs," an even split on columns, and "highly dispersed" line-length and font-size preferences — mean ≈100–120 mm line length, ≈12 pt type size **"but without specification of typeface."** No typeface differed on speed, comprehension, or recall, so "no special type font is suggested to use in print and everyone is free to choose it themselves." Compare [[sources/accelerating-adult-readers-typeface]] (no consensus font, N=60, 51% within-person spread) and the recommended sets in [[sources/font-type-screen-readability-dyslexia]] / [[sources/good-fonts-for-dyslexia]]. See [[concepts/serif-vs-sans]], [[concepts/font-type-typeface]].
- **★ Cross-script transfer is explicitly denied, and that explains the dyslexia non-replication.** Tarasov: "very few researchers have examined non-English texts… guidelines cannot be simply applied to non-English script." So the English (Helvetica/CMU/Arial), Turkish (BonvenoCF/TTKB) and Italian (no winner) sets were *never expected* to agree — see [[concepts/dyslexia-font-recommendations]].
- **★ Metacognitive calibration on screen is now contested, and the prior claim was over-stated.** This hub previously recorded, from [[sources/clinton-2019-paper-vs-screens-meta]], that metacognitive calibration is **worse on screen**. [[sources/halamish-elbaz-2020-childrens-comprehension-metacomprehension]] found metacomprehension judgments **numerically higher on screen but not significant** (t(37) = 1.38, p = .177, d = .21), and [[sources/florit-2025-first-grade-comprehension-monitoring]] found children's *reported* comprehension tracked actual comprehension **regardless of medium**, even while actual comprehension differed. **No study in the corpus shows monitoring tracking medium.** The null does not overturn the earlier claim, but the honest position is that the calibration effect is **unconfirmed here, not established** — and note Halamish's positive comprehension result sits at its own power floor (d = .41 vs a detectable minimum of d ≥ .47). See [[concepts/metacognitive-calibration]].
- **★ Genre: predicted interaction not found where it was expected.** [[sources/mangen-walgermo-bronnick-2013-paper-screen-comprehension]] found **no genre × medium interaction** (p = .707), contradicting the expectation that expository text suffers more on screen; Florit et al. found only a **small** interaction, and only on main-point and literal questions (η² = .02–.04), null for inferential (p = .69). Same variable, different ages, opposite-leaning results.
- **★ Mangen has a design confound worth stating plainly:** both paper and screen groups **took the comprehension test on screen**, so only the *encoding* medium varied. This has been true of this literature since 2013 and has not been designed out.
- **★ Disfluency costs performance without producing reflection.** [[sources/janouskova-2022-font-readability-moses-illusion]]: the difficult font **lowered factual accuracy** (93% vs 77%, p = .039) while leaving error detection unchanged (43% vs 37%, p = .569, **negligible by equivalence testing**). If disfluency's benefit ran through deeper processing, error catching should have improved. It did not. This weakens the "disfluent type aids memory" claim mechanistically, not merely as another null. See [[concepts/disfluency]].
- **★ Familiarity fails as the explanation for typeface preference.** [[sources/ho-sang-petrarca-2025-beyond-typeface-value]] is the only source that manipulates familiarity as a factor: familiar faces were **preferred** but produced **no speed advantage**, and familiar faces did **not** improve **recall**. This directly contradicts Tarasov's "the point to pay attention to is the familiarity of subjects with special typefaces," and reframes the preference result above: people prefer familiar fonts, and that preference buys nothing. See [[concepts/expertise-familiarity]].
- **★ A grey-literature source that contradicts the cluster is refuted by its own data.** [[sources/tajuddin-mohamad-paper-versus-screen-university]] claims screen-superiority for comprehension, but is an undated un-peer-reviewed ProQuest thesis whose own tables report **p = .72**. Retained as a documented reporting failure, **not** as counter-evidence. See [[concepts/methodology-critique]].
- **★ Review-level evidence on what can be measured at all.** [[sources/arya-2023-assessment-methods-readability-legibility]] (PRISMA, 49 studies, 2015–2023) catalogs the field into three method families — formula-based, eye-tracking, subjective rating — and concludes that **traditional readability formulas do not capture modern digital typography**. Independently confirms this hub's recurring lesson in [[concepts/method-type-divergence]]. See [[syntheses/legibility-measurement-standard]].
- **Disfluency debate:** "desirable difficulty" (disfluent type aids memory) is challenged — [[sources/ssrn-5678368]] finds Bayesian null for several typographic manipulations. Flag against any Skimming/Comprehension source that claims disfluency aids retention. See [[concepts/disfluency]].
- **Method-type divergence:** spacing changes comfort (self-report) without changing speed/comprehension (behavioral) [[sources/interletter-line-spacing-reading-speed-comprehension]]; purpose changes think-aloud but not test scores [[sources/journal-of-educational-psychology-1999]]. See [[concepts/method-type-divergence]].
- **Recommendation vs. individualization (the sharpest contradiction in the corpus).** [[sources/font-type-screen-readability-dyslexia]] recommends a specific accessible font set (Helvetica, CMU Sans Serif, Arial) and [[sources/good-fonts-for-dyslexia]] offers a reduced set; [[sources/validating-personalized-visual-auditory-parameters-dyslexia]] explicitly declines to rank and finds **no font selected by more than ~20%** of children, with **every child** improving under their own parameters. Same broad population, opposite conclusions. See [[concepts/dyslexia-font-recommendations]].
- **Preference ≠ effectiveness.** 41% of readers scored their *lowest* comprehension in their preferred font; only 18% read fastest in it; yet 80% believed their preferred font was best for them [[sources/accelerating-adult-readers-typeface]]. Contradicts the intuition behind any user-facing "pick your font" affordance. See [[concepts/preference-versus-effectiveness]].
- **Adaptation is not uniformly good.** Domain-dependent simulated results: computer science significantly better, inorganic chemistry inconclusive, **general biology neutral to slightly negative** [[sources/adaptive-personalization-educational-readings-simulated]]. Meanwhile context-adaptive typography improved goodput without touching comprehension [[sources/situfont-adaptive-mobile-typography-svi]] — whose own limitations note adjustments can fire *unintentionally* during scrolling, turning adaptation into an interruption. Neither "adaptive = better" nor "no evidence of harm" is supportable.
- **Speed vs. fixation-duration.** Typeface effects are robust on fixation duration and marginal/absent on **reading time** — the outcome readers experience. Only 16/66 pairwise comparisons were significant in the 2013 study, and its 2016 revision found the italic penalty absent within dyslexia. Flag any claim of the form "font X is more legible" that rests on fixation duration alone.
- **Vertical vs. horizontal is contested even within one paper.** The Chinese-poster study's own literature review reports conflicting findings — some work favoring vertical for short text, others favoring horizontal for long-text memory and speed. Treat [[sources/chinese-typography-eye-tracking-vertical-horizontal]] as evidence about *posters with imagery*, not as a general rule about reading direction. See [[concepts/text-direction]].
- **Field-level gap:** [[sources/readability-research-an-interdisciplinary-approach]] argues pleasure/preference/engagement are neglected relative to speed and accuracy — yet the corpus evidence above shows preference actively *mispredicts* performance. Both can hold (preference may matter for sustained voluntary reading while failing as a comprehension predictor), but the corpus cannot yet distinguish them.

## Key open questions
- **★ Can a screen-type moderator be estimated at all?** Kong's null may be an artifact of averaging computer and portable devices, since Chen et al. find the split *within* one study. Re-analysis with device class as a pre-specified moderator is the highest-value cheap study in this hub.
- **★ Is the screen→paper comprehension penalty shrinking?** The hypothesis rests on one non-significant coefficient (**b = .26, p = .09** for Year). It must not be cited as evidence of change. A pre-registered meta-regression is needed.
- **★ Does any typography study use a comprehension test independent of the reading medium?** Mangen's confound is 13 years old and persists.
- **★ Which cue does the work?** Lege's 21-bundle cannot answer it, and no source in the corpus isolates heading size, bold, colour, or baseline shift individually in a comprehension outcome.
- **Do legibility gains (speed) ever convert into comprehension gains, or only free resources when text is hard?** The review's answer is that typography is more sensitive to speed than to comprehension/recall [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]] — so the conversion question is largely settled against typography, and the remaining question is about *structure*.
- **Can the typeface question be answered at all?** Re-run it under a shared measurement standard (x-height in mm, leading as fraction of x-height, ISO 3664:2009 lighting, 0.4 m viewing distance). Until then the non-replication is uninterpretable. See [[concepts/methodology-critique]].
- Does structure-aligned formatting generalize beyond expository/EFL samples? **Note that the new Lege evidence is itself EFL-only**, so the corpus's best structural evidence and its weakest population now coincide.
- How do cpl × eye-movement interactions play out on modern mobile screens?
- **Which predictor of an individual's optimal format?** No source identifies one; Wallace et al. pose this as the open question and it remains open. Ho Sang & Petrarca add that familiarity is *not* it. See [[concepts/individual-differences-in-readability]].
- **Do personalization gains persist** beyond the adaptation session, and do they transfer to real reading materials rather than nonwords/pseudosentences?
- **Is there any font effect at all once comprehension is measured properly?** No recommendation study in the corpus measured comprehension.
- **Does prosodic structure drive reading behavior in ordinary prose**, or is it a poetic phenomenon? The syllable-count effect is currently verse-only. See [[concepts/rhythm-rhyme-and-prosody]].
- **What is the cost of unwanted adaptation?** Quantified nowhere, despite one paper flagging it as a real failure mode.
- **Can EOG or simulated readers be validated against human reading** at the level of the individual-variance findings above? See [[concepts/simulated-readers]].
- **Is anything known about comprehension of longer texts?** The new cluster's comprehension studies run 140 words to a few pages; nothing in the corpus tests multi-page or book-length material for comprehension effects. See [[concepts/screen-size]].

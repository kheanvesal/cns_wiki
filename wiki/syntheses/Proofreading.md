# Proofreading — Reading-Task Hub

How text style, layout, and medium affect **error detection** — spotting typos, misspellings, and deviations. Proofreading rewards *slow, surface-level* attention, so it can respond to style variables differently from comprehension (which rewards meaning-building). Two proofreading modes recur: **comparison** (dead copy vs. live copy) and **noncomparison** [[sources/chan-2011-optimum-interface-chinese-proofreading]].

## What the layout evidence says (screen)
- **Line spacing & line number matter most.** Significant main + interaction effects on both proofreading time and detection rate for Chinese screen text [[sources/chan-2014-line-length-spacing-proofreading]]; eye-tracking confirms line spacing reshapes fixation patterns [[sources/huang-2017-line-spacing-simplified-chinese]].
- **Line length & line number drive scrolling effort** — a cost distinct from accuracy [[sources/chan-2014-line-length-spacing-proofreading]].
- **Multiple display factors** (typeface, font size, text direction, copy placement, whitespace/margins) jointly shape speed/accuracy/preference [[sources/chan-2011-optimum-interface-chinese-proofreading]] · [[sources/chan-ng-display-factors-chinese-proofreading]] · [[sources/2534-handheld-whitespace-reading]].

## ★ The strongest single-source result in this hub: line length alone, ω² = .42
[[sources/porte-2001-typographical-error-salience-l2]] deserves to be read as the hub's anchor, because it is the only study in the corpus that **manipulates one typographic variable, holds everything else constant, and measures recognition directly**.

- **Setup:** 60 Spanish L2 EFL undergraduates, all classified as underachieving, **none ever taught proofreading**; 15 implanted spelling errors drawn from *their own* written errors over the previous 9 months (8 categories); ~172-word text; font, size, spacing, print quality and margins all fixed; a frame masked everything outside a highlighted **4-line** section, 30 s per section.
- **Result:** recognition rose from ≈75 cpl (M = 6.33/15) to ≈60 (7.87) to a **peak at ≈45 (8.80)**, then **fell at ≈35 (7.67)**. **ω² = .42**, F = 15.293, p < .001 — 42% of the variance in error recognition is attributable to typographical condition. Post-hoc: C1–C3 = 2.47*** (largest); **C3–C4 = 1.13* (P = .031)**.
- **The inverted U is the contribution.** Line length is not "shorter is better." It helps until the text starts to break up — Porte's "mutilation." Caveat: that turn rests on one 15-person cell and is a post-hoc reading.
- **Convergence with the craft/print norm:** the ≈45 peak is the *lower edge* of both the 45–75 cpl craft range and the range in [[sources/nanavati-bias-2005-optimal-line-length]], a review written about a century earlier for a different technology. See [[concepts/line-length]].

## ★ The ceiling that layout cannot lift
Despite ω² = .42, Porte's **grand mean was 7.67 of 15 — a ~51% detection rate** — on errors the participants had made and been flagged for repeatedly. Layout moves detection substantially and still leaves roughly half of one's own recurring errors uncaught. The more transferable finding is behavioral: 21 subjects finger-traced word-by-word, 18 read aloud, 11 more mouthed text *sotto voce*, and in **38 sessions** participants sensed something was wrong without locating it (**counts only, no statistics applied**).

**Salience is never measured directly in this corpus.** No proofreading study here uses eye-tracking or a perceptual-span measure; "salience" is a label attached to an inference from accuracy. That gap is why the ω² result, however large, cannot be attributed to a mechanism. See [[concepts/error-detection]], [[concepts/methodology-critique]].

## ★ A case where the cost appeared and the benefit did not
[[sources/janouskova-2022-font-readability-moses-illusion]] tests whether harder-to-read text promotes error catching. The difficult font **lowered factual accuracy** (93% vs 77%, z = 2.06, p = .039) while **leaving illusion-error detection unchanged** (43% vs 37%, z = −0.57, p = .569) — established as **negligible by equivalence testing**, the only such use in this corpus. So disfluency **cost accuracy without buying reflection**, in a failed replication of Song & Schwarz (2008) *in the paradigm where the original was found*.

Cross-referenced with Porte, this suggests error type matters: typography may aid **surface orthographic** deviance (Porte) while doing nothing for **meaning-based** deviance (Janoušková). No study here crosses the two error types — an open question, not a finding.

## Contradictions / cautions
- **★ Line length: inverted U, not a monotone preference.** The hub's previously-listed line-length evidence ([[sources/chan-2014-line-length-spacing-proofreading]]) concerns Chinese screen text and does not establish an optimum. Porte establishes one — at **≈45 cpl**, with a **decline at 35**. Anyone citing this hub for "shorter lines aid proofreading" should carry the turn. See [[concepts/line-length]].
- **★ The corpus's largest cue-style effect has the weakest internal validity, and its cleanest causal claim has the smallest effect.** [[sources/porte-2001-typographical-error-salience-l2]] isolates one variable and gets ω² = .42 with n = 15/cell. [[sources/lege-2019-typography-efl-eye-tracking]] applies **21 simultaneous typographic changes** and gets p < .001 on recall with n = 45 after tracking loss — and therefore cannot support any claim about *which* cue did the work. Neither should be read as stronger than its design allows. See [[concepts/signaling]].
- **Script generalization:** most layout evidence here is **Chinese** (Chan/CityU group) — line-spacing/line-number effects may not transfer to alphabetic scripts. Flag before generalizing. See [[entities/alan-chan-cityu-group]].
- **Unresolved source:** [[sources/out-scanned-preview]] (needs OCR/original). **Miscategorized?** [[sources/7085c1aa-book-review]] is a data-graphing book review, not a reading study — LINT candidate.
- **Documented reporting failure:** [[sources/tajuddin-mohamad-paper-versus-screen-university]] (ProQuest thesis, undated, un-peer-reviewed) claims paper-inferiority in proofreading, but its own tables give **p = .72**. It is refuted by its own data and must not be cited as counter-evidence to [[sources/vitello-2022-screen-vs-paper]]. See [[concepts/methodology-critique]].

- **The field's leading variable may be mismeasured.** The century-scale print review argues spacing results are not comparable across studies because leading was reported in uncoordinated units; it requires line spacing **as a fraction of x-height**, plus x-height in mm and lighting per ISO 3664:2009 [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]]. The Chan/CityU line-spacing findings above were produced without that normalization, so they cannot be pooled with other spacing work. See [[concepts/methodology-critique]].
- **Return-sweep mechanism, unmeasured.** Leading controls the eye's sweep back to the start of the next line -- the movement proofreading depends on most -- so spacing plausibly matters more here than for comprehension, and non-monotonically ("not too much or too little") [[sources/tarasov-legibility-of-textbooks-2015]]. Mechanistic account only; not tested by the proofreading studies themselves. **Porte's masking frame is a second, independent route to the same mechanism** — restricting each sweep to 4 lines concentrates attention on surface deviance instead of 2–3 lexical items for meaning, which is the perceptual-span account made operational. Nobody has tested this since 2001.
- **Pooling is still blocked by units.** Porte reports cpl; Tarasov requires mm and x-height fractions. These cannot be reconciled without the typeface specification, which Porte does not give. See [[syntheses/legibility-measurement-standard]].

## Open questions
- Do the Chinese line-spacing/line-number effects replicate for English/alphabetic proofreading?
- **Does Porte's ω² = .42 replicate?** n = 15/cell, one text, one 15-error set, one 15-minute population of underachieving L2 writers, and a 2001 publication date. This is the hub's largest effect size and it has never been independently reproduced. Highest-value open question in this hub.
- **Is the ≈45 cpl peak task-general or proofreading-specific?** It matches the craft norm's lower bound, which suggests a perceptual-span limit rather than a proofreading effect — but no study varies line length across tasks with detection held as the outcome.
- **Does typography help surface error detection and meaning-based error detection equally?** Porte (orthographic) and Janoušková (semantic) point in opposite directions and no study crosses the error types. See [[concepts/error-detection]].
- Can any proofreading study measure *salience* directly — eye-tracking or perceptual span — rather than inferring it from accuracy?
- Does screen's proofreading penalty shrink with modern high-DPI displays?
- Which single display factor best predicts detection rate across scripts?
